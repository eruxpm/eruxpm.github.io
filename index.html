How I think

I work on the gap|between people and systems

Products rarely fail because a feature is missing. They fail in two places: when people can't tell what to do, and when they can't tell what just happened.

Execution. The distance between what someone wants and the controls the product gives them.
Evaluation. The distance between what the system did and what the person understands.

I start by finding where these gaps are widest, then decide which bridge is worth building first. That is where product management and HCI meet: every span has a cost, and the evidence sets the order.

How I think

Why I don't pick one

ProductResearchUser experience

People often ask whether I'm a product person or a UX person. I work across product, research and user experience because none of them holds on its own.

They behave like Borromean rings: no two are linked, yet the three can't be pulled apart. Take one away and the other two fall free. Underneath all three sits human–computer interaction, the study of how people perceive, decide and act with computers. Drag the rings to turn them.

Remove one ring to see what happens to the other two.

Systems thinking

I read the system|below the surface

Systems thinking looks at the whole: the elements, the connections between them, and the purpose they serve together. Behaviour comes from that structure, which is why problems keep coming back when only the symptoms get fixed.

The iceberg model is how I put it to work. Events are what everyone sees. Patterns are what keeps happening. Structures, such as incentives, processes and feedback loops, produce the patterns. Mental models hold the structures in place.

Products, markets and organizations are all systems in this sense. I read them the same way, and I look for the lever below the surface, where a change lasts.

How I work

Every project runs|through the same four rooms

My titles have changed: product manager, researcher, designer, organizational designer, now CPO. The way I work hasn't. Every project moves through the same four rooms, and the loop doesn't stop at launch.

Research

The gap|wasn't fear

Insurancemarket wanted to understand why AI tools hadn't spread across the team as expected. The answer turned out to be in the systems around people, not in the people.

Client
Insurancemarket, insurance comparison, Greece
My role
Analysis and synthesis
Method
Reflexive thematic analysis
Data
11 interviews, in Greek

The brief

Insurancemarket wanted its teams to work with AI the way Advisable's teams work with Claude. The starting assumption was that the gap had to do with people: knowledge, mindset, perhaps fear of AI.

I treated that as a hypothesis to test, not a fact. The stated objective was really a list of interview questions. I rewrote it into four research objectives the analysis could answer:

  1. How work gets done today.
  2. Where effort is wasted.
  3. How people relate to AI.
  4. What past changes say about whether a programme would stick.

Method

From 11 interviews|to 5 themes

Colleagues ran eleven semi-structured interviews on site. I did the analysis, following Braun and Clarke's six phases with Saldaña's coding methods.

  1. Checked transcript integrity and interview quality first, and flagged answers the moderator had led.
  2. Coded every codeable turn: about 390 first-cycle codes in a versioned codebook.
  3. Re-coded the first participants once the codebook had matured.
  4. Grouped the codes into 10 pattern codes, then 5 themes, and reviewed each theme against the whole corpus.
  5. Kept the senior leader's interview apart, as the benchmark to test the staff evidence against.

No inter-coder agreement score (Cohen's kappa): with a single analyst it would be a false signal of rigour. Instead, every claim traces back to a numbered turn in the transcripts.

Findings

Structural,|not personal

What we found

Fear did not explain the adoption gap. Three things held them back: a rigid reporting tool, missing licences and access, and no words for the automation they needed. They blamed themselves for it. The most capable ones quietly rebuilt what was missing in private spreadsheets and personal accounts. There, the company couldn't see it or keep it.

Five themes · share of the 10 participants

  • Data comes in the wrong shape, so people rebuild it by hand9/10
  • The obstacles are concrete and fixable, not fear or skill9/10
  • The real AI capability is private and invisible9/10
  • A deliberate, healthy line around work that should stay human8/10
  • People bypass the official process because the informal one is faster5/10

Outcome

Fix the system,|not the people

Recommendations

Instead of "fear of AI" training, six recommendations, ordered by impact:

  1. Fix the data layer first, so people can reshape their own reports.
  2. Clear the licence, access and account blockers.
  3. Teach people how to describe an automation, not how to "use AI".
  4. Turn private setups into shared tools, and make "shared" part of done.
  5. Make the official path faster than the workaround before adding tools.
  6. Keep automation on the mechanical side of the line people already draw.

From themes to options

From the themes I built an opportunity solution tree, which links each opportunity to possible solutions. For each solution it names the riskiest assumption and the cheapest test. A tension framework then turned contradictions in the evidence into ideas.

Research

Before the|first screen

Before designing X-Shop, I ran a research track on people who build online stores. What do they value, what frustrates them, and where could a new builder do better?

Product
X-Shop, AI-native e-commerce builder
My role
Product, acting Head of UX
Method
User interviews, thematic and content analysis, survey statistics
Status
x-shop.com ↗

Also from this project: UX · the builder →

The brief

X-Shop generates a complete store from a single prompt, in a market led by Shopify, Wix and Webflow. To decide what X-Shop should be, we needed evidence about the people using those tools. A feature comparison was not enough.

I analysed 20 user interviews, alongside desk research on the tools people already use.

Method

Three sources,|one picture

  1. Content analysis of long video reviews of seven builders, from Shopify and Wix to Webflow. I transcribed and coded them, then tallied how often and how strongly each point came up.
  2. Thematic analysis of 20 user interviews, to find customer problems and set priorities: 19 "like" and 11 "dislike" themes for Webflow alone.
  3. A statistical check on survey data: margins of error and confidence intervals, so small samples didn't drive decisions.
  4. An opportunity solution tree: eight opportunities in the users' own words, and a short list of quick wins.

Findings

Two kinds of user,|not one

What we found

Someone opening a shop to sell shoes wants a store online fast, without learning anything. A professional wants Webflow-level control. They struggle with Webflow’s best-known weak spots: class management, dropping into code, and large projects that slow down. E-commerce was rarely the reason anyone chose a builder.

One product for both would fail both. The research turned into three principles:

  1. Progressive disclosure: a simple mode and an advanced mode, with help in context.
  2. Composable parts: templates and complex components that stay fully editable.
  3. Performance always visible: live hints, modular loading, page-by-page publishing.

What it led to

Two builders sharing one store manager: a simple one for shop owners and an advanced one for professionals. How I designed them is in the UX part of this project.

An e-commerce builder designed for two very different kinds of user, on top of one shared store manager.

Product
X-Shop, AI-native e-commerce builder
My role
Product, acting Head of UX
Scope
Information architecture, flows, builder and store manager UI
Status
x-shop.com ↗

Also from this project: Research · before the first screen →

The problem

X-Shop started from the founders’ own work. As developers, they kept building online stores for clients and wanted a faster way to do it. The research showed two users with opposite needs. A shop owner wants a store online fast, without learning anything. A professional wants full control, and struggles with class management, dropping into code and large projects that slow down.

Key decisions

Power without|the learning curve

Two modes, one product

A simple builder for shop owners, with widgets, safe defaults and locked layout rails, and an advanced builder for professionals.

Why: progressive disclosure: beginners see only what they need, experts get depth without it getting in the way.

Styles you can trace

In the advanced builder, a class system shows where every style comes from. People build interactions as visual if/then flows instead of code.

Why: making the system’s state visible prevents accidental overrides, and recognising options is easier than recalling code.

Safe publishing

Breakpoints edited in place, and publishing one page at a time with fast rollback.

Why: large sites become manageable, and any mistake can be undone in one step.

AI where it earns its place

I set up a framework for deciding where AI belongs: a feature needed a user problem first. In the shared store manager, the AI answers questions and runs actions on the catalogue and orders.

Why: AI added without a user need adds complexity without value.

UX

Two roads,|one front door

A redesign of the Fast Pass site and its whole sign-up process. It is built around the one choice that changes everything else: which road you drive on.

Product
Fast Pass by Terna, electronic toll subscriptions, Greece
My role
UX lead: architecture, flows, content, accessibility
Scope
Public site, application, customer account, back office
Status
On delivery

The problem

People didn’t realise there were two roads. A Fast Pass transponder works on every Greek motorway, but the discounts apply only on the two motorways the group runs. A separate company operates each one, with its own programmes, prices and contracts. Signing up was also a paper process, spread across several systems.

Architecture

The road decides|the rest

Road first

The homepage map asks which road people drive on before anything else. That choice sets the company, programmes, prices and contacts on every page that follows.

Why: the first step clears up the most common misunderstanding, and people never see programmes that don’t apply to them.

Savings, not percentages

Each programme appears as a discount, compared side by side. A calculator turns it into the amount a driver saves.

Why: people compare money, not rules. A personal figure is easier to judge than a discount table.

Change without starting over

The five-step application lets people change road or programme at any point without losing what they entered. It only offers the vehicle categories a programme allows.

Why: mistakes are expected, so going back costs nothing. The form prevents invalid options instead of rejecting them later.

Paperless end to end

Customers sign contracts online, and the contracts reach the company’s CRM through APIs.

Why: every manual hand-off was a place where applications stalled or data was retyped wrongly.

Back office

One price list,|every number

Single source of truth

Each company gets a price list editor with dated tariff sheets. A sheet holds the base price per station and vehicle category, plus each programme’s discount rule. Programme pages, station tables, the calculator and customer accounts all read from it.

Why: prices change often and used to be edited by hand page by page. One sheet changed once removes a whole class of errors.

Back office

Requests,|not free edits

Changes as requests

Customers don’t edit sensitive data directly. A plate change, a new transponder or a programme change becomes a request. Staff approve or reject it, seeing the exact before and after.

Why: this data is tied to contracts and billing. Requests keep customers in control while the company keeps its records correct.

Content

Plain words,|clear rules

Copy

Plain wording throughout. The same thing always has the same name, from the programmes to the application and the account.

Why: inconsistent terms make people wonder whether two words mean two different things.

Accessibility

The web app targets WCAG 2.1 AA, and I wrote the public accessibility statement.

UX

A workbench,|not a printout

A redesign of the screens pharmacy staff use all day, so they read faster and act sooner.

Product
e-Syntagi, all-in-one platform for pharmacies
My role
UX lead
Scope
Navigation, dashboard, prescriptions, research approach
Status
esyntagi.gr ↗

The problem

Greek pharmacists dispense prescriptions, take payments and bill the insurance funds through several state systems. e-Syntagi brings them into one place. The product had grown feature by feature around those systems, not around the work at the counter. The result: dense text, scattered actions and a menu that didn’t match how staff think about their day.

Approach

Learning|the domain

With the CEO, a pharmacist

I mapped how a pharmacy really runs, edge cases included: the counter, prescriptions, stock and the monthly insurance claim. I also reshaped the research approach, so what we collected could support product decisions.

Navigation

The menu is|the map

Grouped by task

I reordered and renamed the menu around pharmacists’ tasks instead of the systems behind them.

Why: people look for a function where their own mental model puts it. Labels in their words make it findable without memorising.

Dashboard

Read the day|at a glance

Four figures, then trends

Four cards for today’s key figures, then revenue over time, top brands and a year-on-year comparison. One set of filters applies to every chart at once.

Why: people read bars and lines faster and more accurately than pie angles or plain lists. One filter set once avoids repeating the same choice.

Prescriptions

Scan, open,|act

A table, then details

Each prescription became a row with the fields staff scan for. A row opens into grouped cards: fund, patient, doctor, prescription, pharmacy and billing.

Why: a table supports scanning many records. Grouping the details into chunks lets staff jump straight to the block they need.

Status in words and colour

Status shown with a label as well as a colour.

Why: colour alone fails people with colour-vision deficiencies and is easy to misread at a glance.

UX

From feed|to first search

A merchant dashboard designed around the first run, from connecting a product feed to the first search. After that, it supports the daily work of tuning results.

Product
Findloom, hosted search for online shops
My role
UX lead
Scope
Merchant dashboard, team roles, public site, accessibility
Status
findloom.com ↗

Also from this project: Product · one search, twelve markets →

The problem

Shops wanted site search that costs less than established tools and understands Greek, including Greeklish (Greek typed in Latin letters) and typos. But before seeing any value, a merchant has to connect a product feed, map its fields and wait for indexing. It is a long, technical path, and people easily give up halfway.

Setup

One hub,|three stages

Always show where you stand

A status strip always shows where the shop stands: no feed, indexing, failed, or live with the product count.

Why: indexing takes time. Visible progress tells people the system is working and what to do next.

First things first

An onboarding checklist with progress, and advanced settings kept out of sight until the first sync is done. Actions are grouped in the order people need them.

Why: fewer choices at the start mean a faster first success. Depth is there when people are ready for it.

See what shoppers see

A search preview shows exactly the results shoppers will get, so every change can be checked straight away.

Why: immediate feedback turns relevance tuning from guesswork into trial and check.

Roles

The riskier the setting,|the higher the role

Four roles, one rule

Owner, admin, editor and viewer: the more a setting can break search, the higher the role it needs. The interface hides actions a role can’t take, instead of showing them disabled.

Why: error prevention by design, and a cleaner screen for people who only need part of the tool.

Public site

Show the engine,|don’t describe it

Mechanisms you can watch

Each product page shows the mechanism at work. On the data pipeline page, a short demo follows a feed through mapping, enrichment with Greek spelling variants and indexing.

Why: search infrastructure is abstract. Seeing it run explains it faster than any paragraph. A visible control pauses the animation for people who find motion distracting.

ROI calculator

The cost of|a failed search

One number that matters

Shop owners enter five monthly figures they know or can estimate, each with a sensible default. The calculator returns one headline result, the yearly revenue at risk, then the details.

Why: defaults give people an answer without knowing every figure. One clear result is easier to act on than a table.

An honest assumption

The calculator states its assumption openly: recovered searches convert at the shop’s normal rate, not the higher rate often claimed.

Why: a conservative, visible assumption makes the result credible.

Accessibility

Readable by|everyone

WCAG 2.1 AA

Skip links and labelled landmarks, visible focus and 44-pixel targets. Screen readers announce status changes, and every chart has a text summary.

Outcome

Faster search,|more sales

Results

In the first month, revenue from search grew by 40%. Average response times stay around 50 milliseconds across millions of products.

Product

Ask your business|a question

Advisable.AI lets a company ask questions about its own data in plain Greek or English, and get answers with real numbers. The data can sit in the ERP, tax records, email, helpdesk or bank accounts. I worked on what it does, who it serves, how it earns trust and how it is priced.

My role
Product lead
Scope
Positioning, integrations, trust model, pricing, onboarding
Status

Starting point

Take a simple question such as "which customers owe us money for more than 60 days?" In most small and mid-sized companies, it means asking accounting, waiting for an export and building a spreadsheet. The data exists, but it sits in separate systems, and getting it out costs days.

Decisions

Where the numbers|already live

  1. Start where Greek companies keep their numbers: the SoftOne ERP, the tax authority's myDATA and Greek banks. Then add email, helpdesk and time tracking.

Live, self-service and fully bilingual, with eleven ready-made integrations. Scheduled reports are in beta.

Starting point

Advisable began as a web agency. It already built internal products and occasionally received external proposals, but each opportunity arrived differently. There was no shared intake, evaluation framework or defined route from a pitch deck to an agreement and working product. I proposed the venture studio branch and designed that route.

Evaluation

Separate the idea|from the evidence

  1. One intake form asks every external founder for the problem, vertical, unique advantage, stage, business model and pitch deck.
  2. I check the deck against the outside world: the market, competitors, claims, founder backgrounds, team commitment and evidence of demand.
  3. A 100-point framework weighs eight factors: problem quality, market size, timing, founder–market fit, competitive advantage, validation, business model and studio fit.
  4. Four decision tiers turn the score into an action: prioritise, refine, investigate further or stop unless the venture changes materially.
  5. I reject weak proposals or compress the strongest evidence into a short recommendation for the studio founders, who make the final go/no-go decision.

Example

A viable core,|a premature leap

The venture

An education founder had already built a tutoring marketplace with WordPress, booking plugins and manual operations. She had proved that students and families would pay for specialist tutors. She came to the studio to add a lifelike AI tutor developed with university partners.

What the evidence said

The marketplace had demand. The AI tutor did not: there was no evidence that students, parents or teachers wanted it; native development would take months; and no technical co-founder shared the risk. The people presented as partners were advisers, not committed founders.

The recommendation

Keep the working marketplace, test nearer-term AI tools for education, collect the data needed for a future native model and move the lifelike tutor to the final stage. The founder initially agreed, then chose to keep the original direction. The studio did not proceed.

Results

A common path|to a decision

Where it stands

In one six-week external intake window in 2025, the studio received 18 pitch decks. It now has a common intake form, a 100-point evaluation framework, documented decision tiers and operating models for internal products, co-founded ventures, paid builds and corporate partnerships. External intake remains occasional; most studio products are still created internally.

Delivery

When a venture proceeds, I help define the proposal, equity or hybrid terms, scope, pricing, requirements and delivery plan. I then work across Product and UX with the studio’s developers. For suitable MVPs, the AI-assisted workflow has reduced the path from scope to working product from roughly five weeks to two.

People before features

Most product problems are human problems first. How people perceive, remember, decide and build habits sets the limits of any interface. So I design from cognition and behaviour, not from a feature list.

Emotion is part of usability too: products people enjoy are products people forgive.

On my shelf: Norman · Whalen · Weinschenk · Evans · Eyal · Behavioural Insights Team

Evidence you can defend

Opinions are cheap and research is easy to fake. I code qualitative data properly and let themes come from the data. I check how much a sample can really support, and say clearly what we don't know yet.

Good research changes a decision. If it can't, it's theatre.

On my shelf: Braun & Clarke · Saldaña · Travis & Hodgson · Cross, Dorst & Roozenburg

Design is a way of knowing

Design is how you turn an existing situation into a preferred one. That holds for an onboarding flow, an org chart or a venture studio.

On my shelf: Herbert A. Simon · Nigel Cross · Peter G. Rowe

Structure makes things usable

Don't make people think. Clear information architecture, honest labels and predictable flows do more for usability than any visual polish. They're also the first thing to break when nobody owns them.

On my shelf: Krug · Garrett · Rosenfeld, Morville & Arango · Hartson & Pyla · Sears & Jacko

Ship small, learn fast

The quickest way to the right product is a small one in front of the people who will use it. AI tools let me turn a validated idea into a working version myself. The loop between learning and shipping takes days, not months.

On my shelf: Laura Klein · Jeff Sutherland
